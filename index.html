Restoration · Relationship · Stewardship

Restoring land. Renewing relationships. Supporting long-term stewardship.

Turtle Island Land Trust works to restore degraded natural and urban places and build pathways toward long-term care. Our approach is grounded in ecological science and, where Indigenous Nations choose to participate, Indigenous knowledge, governance and stewardship.

How we work

Restore. Steward. Learn.

Our work repairs ecological damage, supports responsible long-term care, and treats learning as reciprocal: from place, from science, from participating Nations and knowledge holders, and through public education.

01

Restore

Repair degraded lands, waters, habitats and properties so ecological function, biodiversity and resilience can recover.

02

Steward

Care for each place through monitoring, maintenance and relationships that respect ecological responsibilities and the people connected to the land.

03

Learn

Learn with the place and the people connected to it, and share appropriate knowledge through research, monitoring, field learning and public education.

Ways of knowing

Seeing a place with more than one eye.

Our approach is informed by Etuaptmumk (Two-Eyed Seeing), a Mi’kmaw guiding principle articulated by Elder Albert Marshall: learning from the strengths of Indigenous ways of knowing and the strengths of Western ways of knowing, and bringing those strengths together where doing so is appropriate.

Turtle Island Land Trust works across the territories of many distinct Indigenous Nations. Etuaptmumk does not replace a Nation’s own laws, knowledge systems, governance or protocols. Each project must be shaped by the Nation and place involved.

Knowledge is not ours simply because it is shared with us.

Indigenous knowledge, cultural information and community data are used, stored and published only according to the permissions, protocols and data-governance arrangements established with the Nation or knowledge holders involved.

Relationships & accountability

Every project should show how decisions are made.

Scientific monitoring remains essential, but accountability is broader than measurement alone. Project reporting will explain the relationships, knowledge, actions and responsibilities shaping the work.

01

Relationship

Whose territory the project is in, who has responsibilities to the place, and how participation is structured.

02

Knowledge

The scientific evidence and any Indigenous knowledge that may appropriately and consensually inform the work.

03

Action

What restoration or stewardship response follows from what is understood about the place.

04

Accountability

Monitoring, governance, permissions, outcomes and the long-term stewardship pathway.
