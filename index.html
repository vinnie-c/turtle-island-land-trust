Restore · Learn · Steward

Restoring places. Learning from them. Supporting long-term stewardship.

Turtle Island Land Trust restores degraded natural and urban places at different scales — from small community interventions to long-term land and habitat projects. We begin with what the place needs, work with the people and knowledge relevant to the project, and plan for care from the beginning.

Restoration-first stewardship

We begin with what a place needs — not with a single model of ownership or partnership.

TILT is designed around ecological restoration, not land accumulation. Projects can range from a small urban pollinator intervention to long-term restoration of land, habitat, water, buildings or larger properties.

The relationships required are proportionate to the project. Not every TILT project requires formal Indigenous participation. A small community-scale initiative may be delivered with a landholder, municipality, volunteers or local partners. Where a project meaningfully involves an Indigenous Nation, Indigenous knowledge, access, cultural use, governance, long-term land stewardship or a potential transfer, TILT’s stronger Indigenous engagement and knowledge-governance standards apply.

Acquisition, stewardship, partnership, community use and future transfer arrangements are tools that may support a restoration objective. They are not treated as ends in themselves.

How we work

Restore. Learn. Steward.

Restoration is the work. Learning shapes how we understand the place and adapt. Stewardship carries responsibility forward through care, maintenance, governance and relationships appropriate to the project.

01

Restore

Repair degraded lands, waters, habitats, urban spaces and properties so ecological function, biodiversity and resilience can recover.

02

Learn

Learn from monitoring, research, place, community experience and — where shared and relevant — Indigenous knowledge, then share appropriate learning through education.

03

Steward

Care for each place through maintenance, monitoring and governance that match the scale, purpose and relationships of the project.

Ways of knowing

No single lens tells us everything about a place.

Our approach to learning is informed by Etuaptmumk (Two-Eyed Seeing), a Mi’kmaw guiding principle articulated by Elder Albert Marshall: learning from the strengths of Indigenous ways of knowing and the strengths of Western ways of knowing, and bringing those strengths together where doing so is appropriate.

This does not mean every TILT project uses Indigenous knowledge or requires formal Indigenous participation. Etuaptmumk informs our organizational humility about knowledge. Where a project does involve an Indigenous Nation or Indigenous knowledge, the Nation’s own laws, protocols, governance and permissions take precedence over any generic framework.

Different projects require different relationships.

A downtown pollinator bed may involve a property holder, volunteers and municipal requirements. A larger land-stewardship project may involve an Indigenous Nation, researchers, landholders and long-term governance. TILT scales its process to the place rather than forcing every project into the same model.

Relationships & accountability

Every project should show how decisions are made.

Accountability is proportionate to the project, but visitors should still be able to understand the need, evidence, partners, action and long-term responsibility behind the work.

01

Need

What is degraded or missing, why restoration is useful, and what public or ecological benefit is being pursued.

02

People & knowledge

The landholders, communities, partners, science and — where relevant and authorized — Indigenous knowledge shaping the work.

03

Action

What restoration, rehabilitation, planting, monitoring or stewardship response is being undertaken.

04

Accountability

Who is responsible, how results are monitored and what care continues after the initial intervention.

Leadership & accountability

Trust should be visible in how we govern.

TILT publishes its federal corporate identity, governance approach and public Board contact, while allowing directors to decide whether detailed profiles are republished on the website.

Our governance model is designed to support transparent public reporting and, where a project creates a meaningful Nation relationship, appropriate Nation-connected participation and privacy by consent.
