Restoration-first stewardship

We do not begin with ownership. We begin with what a place needs.

TILT is designed around ecological restoration, not land accumulation. A project starts by understanding degradation, ecological function, relationships to territory, and the people with responsibilities to the place.

Restoration is not treated as a reason to exclude Indigenous people from land by default. Where a Nation is involved, access, harvesting, cultural use, co-stewardship and Nation-led governance are matters to be discussed with that Nation and reflected in the project where lawful, safe and agreed.

Acquisition, stewardship, partnership or a future transfer arrangement are tools that may support the restoration objective. They are not treated as ends in themselves.

How we work

Restore. Learn. Steward.

Restoration is the work. Learning shapes how we understand the place and adapt. Stewardship carries responsibility forward through long-term care, governance and relationships.

01

Restore

Repair degraded lands, waters, habitats and properties so ecological function, biodiversity and resilience can recover.

02

Learn

Learn with the place and the people connected to it, and share appropriate knowledge through research, monitoring, field learning and public education.

03

Steward

Care for each place through monitoring, maintenance and relationships that respect ecological responsibilities and the people connected to the land.

Ways of knowing

Seeing a place with more than one eye.

Our approach is informed by Etuaptmumk (Two-Eyed Seeing), a Mi’kmaw guiding principle articulated by Elder Albert Marshall: learning from the strengths of Indigenous ways of knowing and the strengths of Western ways of knowing, and bringing those strengths together where doing so is appropriate.

Turtle Island Land Trust may work across the territories of many distinct Indigenous Nations. Etuaptmumk does not replace a Nation’s own laws, knowledge systems, governance or protocols. Each project must be shaped by the Nation and place involved.

Knowledge is not ours simply because it is shared with us.

Indigenous knowledge, cultural information and community data are used, stored and published only according to the permissions, protocols and data-governance arrangements established with the Nation or knowledge holders involved.

Relationships & accountability

Every project should show how decisions are made.

Scientific monitoring remains essential, but accountability is broader than measurement alone. Project reporting will explain the relationships, knowledge, actions and responsibilities shaping the work.

01

Relationship

Whose territory the project is in, who has responsibilities to the place, and how participation is structured.

02

Knowledge

The scientific evidence and any Indigenous knowledge that may appropriately and consensually inform the work.

03

Action

What restoration or stewardship response follows from what is understood about the place.

04

Accountability

Monitoring, governance, permissions, outcomes and the long-term stewardship pathway.

Leadership & accountability

Trust should be visible in how we govern.

TILT publishes its federal corporate identity, governance approach and public Board contact, while allowing directors to decide whether detailed profiles are republished on the website.

Our governance model is being built to support Nation-connected participation in project territories, privacy by consent, and transparent public reporting as the Trust’s work develops.
