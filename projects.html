Our Work

TILT works at different scales. A project may be a pollinator bed created in a donated parking space, an urban lot, a building, a wetland, a shoreline or a larger land-stewardship project. The process should fit the place rather than forcing every project into the same model.

Project scale

Small interventions and long-term land projects can belong in the same restoration-first organization.

What stays constant is the public-benefit purpose: repair ecological damage, learn from the work and support responsible stewardship. What changes is the level of due diligence, partnership, governance, monitoring and long-term commitment the project requires.

01Community-scale

Pollinator beds, native planting, small habitat interventions, clean-ups and other practical urban or neighbourhood projects.

02Property-scale

Urban lots, buildings, shorelines, wetlands or other properties requiring a deeper restoration and stewardship plan.

03Land & landscape

Larger or long-term projects involving land, habitat, watersheds, governance, acquisition or enduring stewardship arrangements.

Ways of knowing in practice

Use the knowledge that is relevant to the project.

TILT’s approach to learning is informed by Etuaptmumk (Two-Eyed Seeing), a Mi’kmaw guiding principle articulated by Elder Albert Marshall. It reminds us that different knowledge systems can contribute distinct strengths without one being used simply to validate the other.

Not every TILT project uses Indigenous knowledge or requires formal Indigenous participation. A small urban pollinator initiative may rely on ecological evidence, a landholder, municipal requirements and volunteers. Where Indigenous knowledge or a meaningful Nation relationship is involved, the Nation’s own laws, protocols, governance and permissions apply.

EVIDENCE

Ecological science & monitoring

Field surveys, monitoring, restoration practice, experimental methods and other evidence can help describe condition, guide interventions and measure change.

RELATIONSHIPS

Place-based & Indigenous knowledge

Local experience, community knowledge and — where shared and authorized — Indigenous knowledge can inform a project without automatically becoming public data or TILT property.

Learning without extraction

Where Indigenous knowledge, cultural information, sensitive locations or community data are involved, TILT follows project-specific permissions and knowledge-governance arrangements. That standard is triggered by the relationship and the project; it is not a claim that every project has an Indigenous component.

Project reporting

Every project should explain what matters at its scale.

A small pollinator bed does not need the same reporting framework as a land acquisition. But every TILT project should make its purpose, action and responsibility understandable.

01Place

Where the work is happening and what kind of place it is.

02Need

What is degraded, missing or at risk and why restoration is useful.

03People & relationships

The landholder, community, municipality, Nation, volunteers, researchers or other partners relevant to the project.

04Knowledge & evidence

The information used to make decisions, including Indigenous knowledge only where shared and authorized.

05Restoration

The ecological repair, rehabilitation, planting or conservation action being undertaken.

06Stewardship

Who remains responsible, what maintenance or monitoring continues and what long-term outcome is intended.

A proportionate process

Match the governance to the project.

A parking-space pollinator bed does not need the same governance structure as a land acquisition, sensitive research project or long-term stewardship arrangement. TILT scales engagement, documentation and oversight to the actual ecological, legal and relationship implications of the work.

01

Place & setting

The ecological and physical setting, from a single urban space to a larger landscape.

02

Need & objective

What problem the project is trying to solve and the public or ecological benefit sought.

03

People & relationships

Who owns, uses, cares for or is affected by the place and what participation is actually needed.

04

Evidence & learning

The science, experience and other knowledge appropriate to the project, including Nation-specific knowledge where relevant and authorized.

05

Restoration

The intervention, timeline, responsibilities, costs and monitoring appropriate to the scale of the work.

06

Stewardship & future

Maintenance, monitoring, governance and any longer-term ownership, community or stewardship arrangement.

Some projects require deeper relationship and governance.

Where a project meaningfully involves an Indigenous Nation, Indigenous knowledge, access, cultural use, sensitive places, long-term land stewardship or a potential transfer, Nation-specific engagement and knowledge-governance standards become part of the project. Smaller community-scale projects may instead be governed by the landholder, municipality, local partners and volunteers.

Rural Projects

Land, water and habitat.

Rural work can include degraded lands, habitats, wetlands, shorelines and watersheds. Some projects may be straightforward ecological restoration; others may involve long-term land stewardship, Indigenous Nation relationships, research, acquisition or more complex governance.

Work stream
RestorationLand

Ecological restoration

Habitat rehabilitation, biodiversity recovery, water and shoreline restoration, invasive-species management, native planting and monitoring shaped by the needs of the place.

Work stream
StewardshipLand

Long-term stewardship

Care, monitoring and governance arrangements intended to endure beyond the restoration phase, including community, conservation or Indigenous stewardship pathways where they are relevant and agreed.

Urban Projects

Small spaces, land and buildings in cities.

Urban restoration can happen at many scales: a single parking space, a vacant lot, a courtyard, a habitat corridor, a heritage property or a building. These projects can be driven by landholders, residents, municipalities, volunteers, institutions or other partners.

Initiative model
RestorationUrbanPollinators

Pollinator parking spaces

Participating landholders or parking-space users can dedicate the use of a downtown parking space to a native pollinator bed. The model turns a very small paved footprint into habitat and a visible public learning opportunity.

Work stream
RestorationLand

Urban land restoration

Vacant or degraded urban land can be restored for biodiversity, ecological function, public learning and long-term community stewardship.

Work stream
RestorationBuildings

Building rehabilitation

Buildings can be restored, conserved or adapted to support educational programming, charitable activities, community use and place-based stewardship.

Education & Learning

Learning is part of restoration.

Projects can support research, field learning, demonstrations, workshops and public education. What gets taught or published depends on the project. Where Indigenous knowledge is involved, it is shared only under the permissions and protocols agreed with the Nation or knowledge holders.
