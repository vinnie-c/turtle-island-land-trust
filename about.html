About Us

We restore degraded places at different scales, learn from the knowledge and experience relevant to the work, and build stewardship arrangements appropriate to each project.

Ways of knowing

No single lens tells us everything about a place.

Our approach to learning is informed by Etuaptmumk (Two-Eyed Seeing), a Mi’kmaw guiding principle articulated by Elder Albert Marshall: learning from the strengths of Indigenous ways of knowing and the strengths of Western ways of knowing, and using those strengths together where appropriate.

This does not mean every TILT project uses Indigenous knowledge or requires formal Indigenous participation. A small urban pollinator project may rely on ecological evidence, a landholder, municipal requirements, volunteers and local experience. Etuaptmumk informs our humility about knowledge rather than imposing a single partnership model on every project.

WHERE RELEVANT

Indigenous ways of knowing

Where Indigenous knowledge is shared and relevant to a project, it can carry relationships, responsibilities, histories, languages, observations and understandings of place developed across generations. What may be used or published is determined through the relevant Nation’s and knowledge holders’ protocols.

ACROSS OUR WORK

Ecological science & learning

Field surveys, monitoring, modelling, restoration practice, experimental work, community experience and other evidence can help describe ecological condition, guide interventions and measure change.

Nation-specific, not pan-Indigenous

Where a project meaningfully involves an Indigenous Nation or Indigenous knowledge, the Nation’s own laws, protocols, governance and permissions take precedence over any generic framework. Etuaptmumk is Mi’kmaw and is not a substitute for Nation-specific approaches.

Knowledge governance when triggered

Indigenous knowledge, cultural information and community data shared in a project do not become TILT property simply because they inform the work. Those protections apply when such knowledge or relationships are actually part of a project; they are not a claim that every TILT project has an Indigenous component.

About the name

Turtle Island is a term used by a number of Indigenous peoples for the lands now commonly called North America, with meanings and traditions that differ among Nations. TILT does not treat the term as a single pan-Indigenous identity and does not claim Indigenous authority by using the name.

Where a project involves an Indigenous Nation, that Nation’s names, laws, protocols, territorial relationships and preferred language take precedence over TILT's organizational name.

When Indigenous engagement is relevant

Not every TILT project requires a formal Nation relationship. Where a project meaningfully involves an Indigenous Nation, Indigenous knowledge, access or cultural use, Nation governance, long-term land stewardship or a potential transfer, TILT begins that relationship before treating major project decisions as settled.

Those projects follow stronger engagement, consent, privacy and knowledge-governance standards appropriate to the Nation and the work.

Our approach

Restore. Learn. Steward.

Restoration is the work. Learning shapes how we understand the place and adapt. Stewardship carries responsibility forward through care, maintenance, governance and relationships proportionate to the project.

01

Restore

Rehabilitate degraded, contaminated, vacant or underused places and recover ecological function, biodiversity, resilience and water quality where possible.

02

Learn

Learn from monitoring, research, place, community experience and — where shared and relevant — Indigenous knowledge, then share appropriate learning through education.

03

Steward

Care for the place through maintenance, monitoring and governance that match the scale, purpose and relationships of the project.

Our mission

Turtle Island Land Trust restores, protects and stewards natural and urban places for public benefit while advancing ecological restoration, biodiversity conservation, research and environmental education.

Our restoration pathway

TILT can work from very small urban interventions to larger land-restoration projects. Institutional ownership is not automatically the goal. Depending on the project, the long-term outcome may be continued TILT stewardship, community or partner stewardship, another conservation arrangement, or — where a participating Indigenous Nation wishes it and legal and project conditions permit — long-term Indigenous stewardship or transfer.

Where we work

The Trust is Canadian in corporate scope. Each project is understood through its actual ecological, physical and community context. Where an Indigenous Nation relationship is materially relevant, territory and Nation-specific context become part of that project’s governance and planning.

What counts as a place

A place may be a single parking space converted to pollinator habitat, an urban lot, courtyard, building, wetland, shoreline, rural property, heritage site or larger landscape. The level of process should match the scale and implications of the work.

Project pathway

Place → Need → People & Knowledge → Restoration → Stewardship

The pathway stays recognizable across TILT’s work, but the depth of due diligence, engagement, governance and monitoring scales with the project.

01

Place

Understand the physical and ecological setting, from a parking space to a landscape.

02

Need

Identify what is degraded, missing or at risk and what restoration could accomplish.

03

People & knowledge

Identify the landholders, communities, partners, evidence and — where relevant — Nations and knowledge required for the work.

04

Restoration

Design and undertake ecological repair appropriate to the scale and purpose of the project.

05

Stewardship

Plan the maintenance, monitoring, responsibilities and long-term future that the project actually needs.

Indigenous governance

When Nation participation is part of the project.

Not every TILT project requires Indigenous governance participation. Where a project materially involves a Nation’s interests, knowledge, access, cultural use, long-term land stewardship or a potential transfer, our goal is to seek meaningful governance participation identified with or supported by that Nation, where the Nation wishes to participate.

TILT does not presume to decide who speaks for a Nation. The appropriate relationship, representative or governance mechanism must be developed with the Nation itself.

For substantial land or stewardship projects, the relationship may include Board participation, project governance, co-stewardship, advisory structures or another arrangement preferred by the Nation.

01

Project-linked participation

Where a Nation is materially involved in a substantial project, the Trust seeks meaningful Board or project-governance participation connected to or supported by that Nation. The form of participation should fit the project and the Nation’s preference.

02

Privacy by consent

Names, photographs, biographies, Nation affiliations and personal contact information are not published on this website without explicit consent. Where legally available, a service address can be used for corporate filings instead of a residential address. Statutory public disclosure requirements still apply.

03

Participation support

The Trust intends to cover reasonable travel, accommodation and necessary meeting costs so participation does not create a financial barrier. We are also committed to not relying on unpaid Indigenous expertise.

04

Respect for expertise

Where funding and applicable charity law permit, compensation may be provided for eligible project, cultural, advisory, professional or other services that are separate from a director’s statutory Board duties.
